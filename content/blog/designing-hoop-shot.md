# Designing Hoop Shot

A basketball game can be extremely simple on paper: aim, shoot, score. The challenge is making those three actions feel responsive and satisfying. **Hoop Shot** gave me a practical way to explore that problem.

## The Player Should Understand the Shot

The trajectory or aiming system needs to communicate what will happen before the player commits. Even when the game does not show a literal prediction line, movement, positioning, and input response should make the relationship between the player's action and the ball clear.

This is a general UI principle: the interface should not make the user guess what their input means.

## Timing Creates Skill

A basketball game becomes more interesting when success depends on timing rather than pure repetition. The player begins by learning the rhythm, then starts adjusting based on distance, angle, and previous results.

That creates a natural learning curve without requiring a long tutorial.

## Making Success Feel Good

A successful shot deserves immediate confirmation. Score changes, motion, and short visual effects can make the result feel important. Failure also needs useful feedback: the player should understand that the shot missed and have a clear opportunity to try again.

The most useful rule I took from this project is simple: **every important action should have a visible consequence.**

## What This Taught Me

Hoop Shot reminded me that product quality is often hidden in micro-interactions. A game can have a small number of screens and still feel polished when input, animation, feedback, and pacing are carefully connected.

That same thinking applies to dashboards, mobile apps, and websites: make actions obvious, responses immediate, and transitions purposeful.
